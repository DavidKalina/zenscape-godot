The Runaway Wheel

motif-m1-runaway-wheel.md · verb chase (a millstone beetle chases you down its lane, then you chase its loose wheel); scale human; mood playful; shape spread wide (three isles a long flight apart).

A millstone beetle rolls its wheel up and down a straight lane, and you lead it to the lip so a timed jump sends the wheel off alone, then learn the game on a grand beetle below whose wheel you cannot jump. Lead the grand one down its lane and sidestep: its wheel tears loose at a cut too wide to jump, jams in it and becomes the only way across.

Fit: role "peak" with a satellite; footprint about 60 m by 24 m (Mill Head) plus 40 m by 20 m (Tail Race) and a far outlier about 190 m east; height span about 20 m (the drop plus the Far Stone's rise).

Play: You walk onto the Mill Head lane, the beetle ticks, and you run before it so its straight charge ends at the east lip; a jump over its wheel lets the wheel tear free and go over. You glide down to the Tail Race, where the grand beetle's wheel is taller than any jump: you lead it down its lane and step aside on the click, and its wheel tears out at the cut and jams. The plug is the only way over (the cut is wider than a long jump with no run-up); too slow or too wide a sidestep and it goes into the void. A loud, cheerful bowling game you are always slightly late for.

Set pieces: - The lane: floor slabs a few charges long and a run wide, stone strips each side; a charge goes down it dead straight, so a step aside is a dodge.
- The lip and its drop: stepped east end whose last step is the commit mark; the first wheel leaves it and goes over alone.
- The grand beetle: a `wheel` 6 millstone (diameter about 4.8 m, nothing to jump), led to the cut and sidestepped.
- The cut: a notch 4.5 m wide (0.95 of the grand wheel's diameter; plain long jump reaches about 4.2 m, LONG_SPEED*2*sqrt(2*LONG_HEIGHT/LONG_GRAVITY)), with only a 2.4 m approach slab behind it so no run-up chains a longer jump; the wheel jams flush and he walks over it.
- The Far Stone's second lane: the same game uphill, a lip over open void, so he may be chased or fly on.

The Ewer Road

motif-m2-ewer-road.md · verb carry; scale intimate; mood quiet; shape among isles a line; world sky.

A dry cistern on the far isle of a line of five small isles is waiting for water, and the only vessel is a stone ewer the pilot fills at the spring by the plane and carries on his head. Every hard move spills it, so the road is a walk taken slowly: the plain jump, the stepping stones, a swaying rope bridge, and a horn that gusts across the last court.

Fit: role stretch (a connecting line of small isles), footprint about 110 by 24 m, height span about 5 m.

Play: He fills the ewer at the spring and sets off with it held on his head: he walks and plain-jumps as ever, but a run, a long jump, a slam, a wall kick or a ledge grab would spill it, so he takes steps, ramps and stones at a walk and sets the ewer down on a lip before a climb, hops up, and lifts it after. The rope bridge sways under him and the walk across has to be steered to its beat; the last court has a wind horn that gusts him. He pours it into the cistern and the cistern gives back a small curtain of water over the rim, a quiet payoff for a slow errand.

Set pieces: - The spring: a worn stone ring on the landing isle; stand at it and the ewer fills over about a second, the fill showing as a level in the jar.
- The stepping stones (isle 2 to 3): four floating stones, two plain jumps wide each; he carries the ewer across at a walk pace, the bob of each stone shaking a little water.
- The swaying rope bridge (3 to 4): a live rope bridge a pilot can cross at a walk without spilling if he steps to its rhythm; run on it and the water slops over the lip.
- The horn court (4): a wind_horn on a pier-backed dais; he waits behind a pier, crosses between gusts, and a gust that lands makes him drop the ewer, which rolls to a stop a few steps off with half its water gone.
- The dry cistern (5): the stone_circle basin; pouring in it starts a trickle that falls off the rim in a thin curtain and wakes the isle's garden.

Five Wicks

motif-m3-five-wicks.md · verb stay hidden; mood oppressive; shape a tight cluster; world under (the Olmec city).

Four great cressets burn round a sunken court on a tight cluster of pillars, and the one headless body that walks its ring is lit as plain as day anywhere in it. Light is what betrays you here, so you put your own torch out, keep to the dark outer faces, and get him to knock the fires over for you.

Fit: role the hero hide place; footprint about 80 x 70 m; height span about 15 m (V's court up to a spire crown, X a course over A).

Play: You step out onto A and the whole court is lit and the body is in it, so you go the other way first: into the snuff trough on A's lip, where your torch hisses out, and you read dark. From there you hug the outer faces of the spires, where the fires' light has died away, and climb a spire's glyph stair to a perch over the ring. A noise from the perch (a slam, a rune) draws him off his beat past a cresset; he barges it, it tips and spills, the pool goes dark and he stands over the spill looking for you, and that is the window. Four fires, four windows, and the last of them opens the way across the court to X, where a lit fire relights your torch.

Set pieces: - The trough (`city_hush`'s snuff on A's north lip): step in and the torch goes out; the lit lamp at X's mouth is the only relight, so you cross the cluster dark and cannot go back lit.
- The wick spires (`maker_wickspire`, 2 courses up): each a perch over its quarter of the ring with a glyph stair to climb; a slam or a rune taken from the top is the lure, loud enough for the beat's far side.
- The tipped cresset (`maker_cresset`, great): on his lane, barged and spilled into a Beacon he looks at for the spill's life, then dark. The spill burns a while, so the window is short, not a rest.
- The court (`city_pillar` crown, sunk a course): bare when dark, a stage when lit. The seams between two pools sum their light, so the seam is the brightest ground in the place.

The Wick (vigil of the great censer)

motif-m4-vigil-of-the-wick.md · Verb defend; Mood sacred; Scale colossal; World sky.

A censer the size of a chapel hangs from a hall column over a ring of stone, and its flame draws every Olmec in the sky toward it along three aqueducts. The pilot has no weapon, so he keeps the vigil by turning the place against them: a bell to call the flame back up, horns to blow bodies off the spans, and a slam to drop a span under them.

Fit: role: set piece / anchor. Footprint: about 200 pilot-widths across the ring plus three spans, each 20-30 pilot-widths long; height span: about 30 pilot heights from Apron to beam walk and bell.

Play: Land on the Apron, walk up the terrace and the flame is already burning; bodies come out of the three spans in turn, slow and pulled toward the bowl. The pilot gets each of them off the span before it reaches the terrace: lure one onto a horn's line and let it gust it off, slam the broken span's loose voussoir so the whole run goes, or wall-kick up a column and flow-charge down onto the one that got through. A body that stands at the bowl for its dwell lowers the flame a notch; ring the bell and the flame climbs back. The feeling is of keeping a very large, very old thing lit.

Set pieces: - The bowl: lit height shown by the flame; each body that reaches it for its dwell drops it a notch. At three notches it goes out and the vigil ends (the Altar goes dark, bodies drift away, you may relight it from the bell).
- The bell on a high perch (cloister side): ringing it lifts the flame one notch and every body on the spans looks up and stops for a count.
- The broken span: a loose voussoir at its mid arch; slammed (down-charge) it drops the span's far half with whatever is on it.
- Two wind_horns on the terrace rim, aimed along two spans: blow a body that stands in their line off the edge.

The Same Floor

motif-m5-same-floor.md · verb fall/descend; mood wrong; scale a fragment of something bigger; world sky.

A slice of a tower pokes out of a plate's east lip and runs on down into the cloud: three rooms stacked, each the very same room (same pier, same chest, same lancet), cut open on one side like a dollhouse. Down is the goal, and the building wants you down: each room's hole is somewhere you did not expect, and the third floor has no floor under it, because the tower goes on without you.

Fit: role set piece/descent; footprint about 90 x 50 m (plate 64 x 40, slice 14 x 14, Base 24 x 20); height span about 40 m.

Play: You walk to the lip, see the rooms, and step in through the missing wall. Each room teaches you its hole, and the next shows the same room with the hole moved, so you cannot run on memory; you read the slab, hang from a hole's lip and let go for a short drop. The third room ends at the cut: you leave it by the pocket sail, out and down to Base, with the whole slice hanging over you like a bell. It leaves a small chill: the place is not broken, it is just not for you, and you are only passing through it.

Set pieces: - The section: three `floor`-slab rooms 14 m square (about 12 pilot heights across), 5.4 m apart (about 4.5 pilot heights, so no floor can be climbed up from the one below; drops are short from a hanging lip). Each has a `pier` in the centre, a `tomb_chest` by the north wall, a `lancet_wall` on the north, and the south or east wall left off as the cut. Built from one recipe stamped three times: only the hole differs.
- The holes: A's hole is the SE corner, B's the NW, C's the centre where its pier stood. Each is about 3 pilot widths, a drop you can hang from and let go (1.2 m fall) or just step into (5 m).
- The eager pier: B's `pier` is a wind blaster's plate; stand in B and its mouths inhale and shove you toward the nearest open side, the cut, where a fall is 22 m down to the cloud. Keep moving and it blasts behind you; stand still and the building posts you out.
- The stub: C's slab ends in a ragged edge with the start of a fourth floor's `wall` and `pier` stumps, the tower still going on under the cloud. The pocket sail opens off the edge; the glide to Base is long and slow, a view of the whole cut.
